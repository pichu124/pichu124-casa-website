Your child chatters away at home but hides behind you when meeting someone new. Or your child talks less than others the same age, and every question about school gets the same answer: "I played". Many parents worry, and hope that a place full of other children will help their child talk more and feel more confident. This article explains what a preschool class can do, what you can do at home, and when to ask a specialist.

## Shyness and late talking are two different things

**Shyness** is temperament. A shy child needs time to watch before joining in, is wary of strangers, and says little in a crowd but plenty with familiar people. It is not your child's fault and not something that needs "fixing". Many shy children grow up to be good listeners and careful thinkers.

**Late talking** is when a child's speech is well behind others the same age, both at home and elsewhere. Every child has their own pace, so a small gap is normal. But if you are worried, do not just wait. Ask a specialist (see the last section).

Some children are both shy and quiet. They usually need extra time and a sense of safety before they start to speak up.

## How does a preschool class help?

- **A reason to talk.** At home, adults often understand a child before they say anything. In class, children need words to ask for a toy, invite a friend to play, or tell the teacher something. Every small need is a chance to practise.
- **Lots of listening every day.** Songs, stories and conversation at meals and playtime give children more words, even if they do not say them back straight away.
- **A steady rhythm.** A familiar daily routine helps a shy child know what comes next, so they worry less and join in more easily.
- **Adults who watch and guide.** The teacher notices when a child is ready, invites them gently into activities, and does not make them speak in front of the class before they want to.

A class will not change a child overnight. A shy child may spend the first few weeks just watching. That is still learning.

## What can you do at home?

- **Talk about what your child is doing.** "You're stacking the red blocks, so tall!" Speak slowly, in short sentences, and leave space for your child to reply.
- **Let your child finish.** Do not speak for them or guess ahead. Count to five silently before helping.
- **Ask open questions about school.** Instead of "did you have fun today?", try "who did you play with today?" or "what did you have for lunch?".
- **Read and tell stories every day.** Let your child point at pictures, name things and guess what happens next.
- **Avoid labels in front of your child.** Saying "my child is very shy" makes a child believe that is who they are. Try "they need a little time to warm up".
- **Limit screen time.** Talking face to face with adults helps children learn to speak more than watching videos alone.

## Talking with the teacher

Tell the teacher about your child: what they like, whether they talk a lot or a little at home, what scares them and what makes them happy. A teacher who knows your child will know when to invite them in.

In turn, ask the teacher who your child usually plays with, whether they seem bolder after a few weeks, and whether there are moments when they talk more than at home. When both sides watch, it is easier to see your child's progress.

## When should you ask a specialist?

A preschool supports children through everyday activities, but it cannot replace a specialist's assessment. Ask a paediatrician or a speech and language specialist if:

- your child talks much less than others the same age, both at home and elsewhere;
- your child rarely responds to their name, or rarely uses gestures such as pointing or waving;
- your child no longer says words they used to say;
- you have a feeling something is not right, even if you cannot name it.

Asking early does not mean something is wrong. If all is well, you can stop worrying. If your child needs extra support, starting early helps more.
